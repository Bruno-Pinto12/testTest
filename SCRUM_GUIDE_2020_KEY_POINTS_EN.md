# Scrum Guide 2020: Key Points

An original-language study summary of the November 2020 Scrum Guide. It is
designed to help with revision, not to replace the official Guide. Where this
document distinguishes a requirement from a common practice, the distinction
is intentional: Scrum defines a framework, not a complete set of procedures.

Official source: [The Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html).

## 1. Scrum at a glance

Scrum is a lightweight framework for people, teams, and organizations to
generate value by adapting solutions to complex problems. It is intentionally
incomplete: it defines the essential parts needed to apply Scrum, while leaving
room for teams to choose suitable techniques and practices.

The framework brings together:

- One Scrum Team: the Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Developers.
- One Product Backlog: the ordered source of work for the Scrum Team.
- A sequence of Sprints: fixed-length periods of one month or less.
- Four events inside each Sprint: Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review,
  and Sprint Retrospective.
- Three artifacts, each with a commitment: Product Backlog / Product Goal,
  Sprint Backlog / Sprint Goal, and Increment / Definition of Done.

Scrum is based on empiricism and lean thinking. The framework is intentionally
incomplete, but Scrum exists only in its entirety: using only selected parts is
possible, but the result is not Scrum. Scrum can contain other techniques,
methodologies, and practices.

## 2. Foundations: empiricism and lean thinking

### Empiricism

Empiricism is the idea that knowledge comes from experience and that decisions
are based on what is observed. It is especially useful when a problem is complex
and the best path is not fully known in advance.

Scrum uses three pillars to support empirical process control:

1. **Transparency** — Important aspects of the process and work are visible to
   those doing the work and those receiving it.
2. **Inspection** — The Scrum artifacts and progress toward agreed goals are
   checked often enough to notice unwanted differences or emerging problems.
3. **Adaptation** — When inspection shows that a process or product is outside
   acceptable limits, it is adjusted promptly.

The pillars reinforce one another: inspection is useful when there is
transparency, and inspection should lead to adaptation when needed.

### Lean thinking

Lean thinking emphasizes reducing waste and concentrating on what is
essential. In Scrum, empiricism and lean thinking complement each other:
frequent feedback helps the team learn, while focus on essentials helps avoid
work that does not contribute enough value.

### Scrum values

The Scrum Team's use of Scrum depends on these five values:

- **Commitment** — Members personally commit to achieving the Scrum Team's
  goals and support one another.
- **Focus** — Members concentrate on the work of the Sprint and the goals of
  the Scrum Team.
- **Openness** — Members and stakeholders are candid about work, progress,
  challenges, and learning.
- **Respect** — Members recognize one another as capable, independent people.
- **Courage** — Members address difficult problems and do the right thing,
  including raising concerns.

The Guide does not assign each value to a particular empiricism pillar. The
values guide the Scrum Team's work, actions, and behavior; they are not a
formal pillar-to-value mapping.

## 3. The Scrum Team

The Scrum Team is a cohesive unit focused on one Product Goal. It has no
sub-teams or internal hierarchies. It is cross-functional, meaning that the
team collectively has the skills needed to create value in each Sprint, and
self-managing, meaning that the team decides internally who does what, when,
and how.

The Guide describes Scrum Teams as typically 10 or fewer people. If a team
becomes too large, the teams should consider reorganizing into multiple cohesive
Scrum Teams focused on the same product. Those teams share the same Product
Goal, Product Backlog, Product Owner, and Definition of Done.

The Scrum Team is accountable for creating a valuable, useful Increment every
Sprint. The three accountabilities within the team are Product Owner, Scrum
Master, and Developers. The team is also responsible for all product-related
activities, including stakeholder collaboration, verification, maintenance,
operation, experimentation, and research and development.

### Product Owner

The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing the value of the product
resulting from the Scrum Team's work and for effective Product Backlog
management. This includes:

- Developing and communicating the Product Goal.
- Creating Product Backlog items and communicating them clearly.
- Ordering the Product Backlog.
- Ensuring that the Product Backlog is transparent, visible, and understood.

The Product Owner may delegate Product Backlog work to others, but remains
accountable for the result. The Product Owner is one person, not a committee.
Stakeholders can influence the Product Backlog by discussing needs and trade-
offs with the Product Owner.

### Developers

Developers are the people in the Scrum Team who commit to creating any aspect
of a usable Increment each Sprint. Their accountabilities include:

- Creating a plan for the Sprint, the Sprint Backlog.
- Instilling quality by adhering to the Definition of Done.
- Adapting the plan each day toward the Sprint Goal.
- Holding one another accountable as professionals.

Developers decide how to turn selected work into a usable, Done Increment;
others do not prescribe how they do this. During Sprint Planning, they
collaborate with the Product Owner on what can be done and why it is valuable.
Developers make the forecast of work for the Sprint; the forecast is not a
guarantee that every selected item will be completed.

### Scrum Master

The Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum as defined in the
Guide and for the Scrum Team's effectiveness. The Scrum Master serves the
Scrum Team, the Product Owner, and the wider organization through coaching,
facilitation when useful, and helping remove impediments to progress.

Examples of Scrum Master service include:

- Coaching team members in self-management and cross-functionality.
- Helping the Scrum Team focus on valuable, Done Increments.
- Ensuring Scrum events happen and are positive, productive, and kept within
  their timeboxes.
- Helping the Product Owner with effective Product Backlog management and
  stakeholder collaboration.
- Helping the organization understand and adopt Scrum, and removing barriers
  between stakeholders and Scrum Teams.

The Guide does not require the Scrum Master to personally facilitate every
event or prescribe one management style. The Scrum Master is accountable for
Scrum being understood and used effectively.

## 4. The Sprint and its events

### Sprint

A Sprint is a fixed-length event of one month or less. A new Sprint starts
immediately after the previous one ends. Sprints provide a consistent rhythm
for achieving goals, inspecting results, and adapting plans.

Each Scrum event is a formal opportunity to inspect and adapt Scrum artifacts
and is designed to enable transparency. Skipping or changing events from the
Guide's prescribed form loses opportunities to inspect and adapt.

During a Sprint:

- No changes are made that would endanger the Sprint Goal.
- Quality does not decrease.
- The Product Backlog is refined as needed.
- Scope may be clarified and renegotiated with the Product Owner as more is
  learned, provided the Sprint Goal is not endangered.

Only the Product Owner can cancel a Sprint, and only when the Sprint Goal
becomes obsolete. A cancellation is unusual because Sprints are short.

The Sprint is the container for the other Scrum events. The Sprint itself has
a maximum duration of one month. Event timeboxes are maximums for a
one-month Sprint; for shorter Sprints, the events are usually shorter. The
Guide does not prescribe exact scaled durations for every shorter Sprint.

### Sprint Planning

Sprint Planning starts the Sprint by setting up the work to be done. The
entire Scrum Team collaborates. The result is the Sprint Backlog, including a
Sprint Goal, the selected Product Backlog items, and a plan for delivering
them.

Planning addresses three concerns:

1. **Why is this Sprint valuable?** The Scrum Team collaborates on a Sprint
   Goal that explains the value of the Sprint.
2. **What can be done this Sprint?** Developers discuss Product Backlog items
   with the Product Owner and make a forecast of what can be completed.
3. **How will the chosen work get done?** Developers plan the work needed to
   create an Increment that meets the Definition of Done.

The Scrum Team collaborates on the Sprint Goal. Developers make the forecast
after discussing the Product Backlog items with the Product Owner. As the work
is better understood, Developers may refine or adjust the plan during the
Sprint.

**Timebox:** At most eight hours for a one-month Sprint. Shorter Sprints
usually have shorter planning.

### Daily Scrum

The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for Developers. Its purpose is to inspect
progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt the Sprint Backlog as necessary,
adjusting the upcoming work plan.

It takes place at the same time and place each working day of the Sprint to
reduce complexity. Developers choose the structure and techniques, as long as
the event focuses on progress toward the Sprint Goal and produces an
actionable plan for the next working day.

The Daily Scrum is not the only time Developers may adjust their plan. They
may meet throughout the day for more detailed discussion. The event is not a
status report to the Scrum Master or Product Owner.

The Product Owner or Scrum Master participate as Developers only when they
are actively working on items in the Sprint Backlog. Otherwise, they do not
participate as part of the Daily Scrum.

### Sprint Review

The Sprint Review is an opportunity for the Scrum Team and stakeholders to
inspect the Sprint's outcome and decide what to do next. The team discusses
progress toward the Product Goal, changes in the environment, and possible
future adaptations to the Product Backlog.

It is a working session, not merely a presentation or a release approval
gate. The Scrum Team does not need to follow a prescribed presentation
format. The Product Backlog may be adjusted to reflect new opportunities or
learning.

**Timebox:** At most four hours for a one-month Sprint. Shorter Sprints
usually have shorter reviews.

### Sprint Retrospective

The Sprint Retrospective is an opportunity for the Scrum Team to plan ways to
increase quality and effectiveness. The team may inspect how the Sprint went
with respect to people, interactions, processes, tools, and the Definition of
Done, and identify the assumptions that led it in certain directions.

The team discusses what went well, what problems occurred, and how those
problems were or were not resolved. The Scrum Team identifies the most helpful
changes to improve its effectiveness; the most impactful improvements are
addressed as soon as possible. Improvements may be added to the Sprint Backlog
for the next Sprint.

**Timebox:** At most three hours for a one-month Sprint. Shorter Sprints
usually have shorter retrospectives.

### Event timebox quick reference

| Event | Maximum for a one-month Sprint |
|---|---:|
| Sprint | One month |
| Sprint Planning | Eight hours |
| Daily Scrum | 15 minutes |
| Sprint Review | Four hours |
| Sprint Retrospective | Three hours |

These are maximums, not targets. The Guide says shorter Sprints usually have
shorter events, but does not set an exact conversion formula for two-week or
other shorter Sprints. Product Backlog refinement is ongoing work, not a
separate Scrum event with a prescribed timebox.

## 5. The artifacts and their commitments

Scrum artifacts represent work or value. They are designed to make important
information transparent so that everyone inspecting them has the same basis
for adaptation. Each artifact has a commitment that helps focus inspection
and progress.

| Artifact | What it represents | Commitment |
|---|---|---|
| Product Backlog | An emergent, ordered list of what is needed to improve the product | Product Goal |
| Sprint Backlog | The Sprint Goal, selected Product Backlog items, and the plan to deliver them | Sprint Goal |
| Increment | A concrete stepping stone toward the Product Goal; the usable result of work meeting the Definition of Done | Definition of Done |

### Product Backlog and Product Goal

The Product Backlog is the single source of work undertaken by the Scrum
Team. It is emergent: it changes as the product and its context are better
understood. Refinement is the ongoing activity of breaking down and further
defining Product Backlog items. Details such as description, order, and size
may be added or changed during refinement.

Developers who will do the work are responsible for sizing it. The Product
Owner may influence Developers by helping them understand trade-offs and
clarifying items. Scrum does not mandate a sizing scale, estimation
technique, or Product Backlog item format.

The **Product Goal** describes a future state of the product and serves as
the Scrum Team's long-term objective. It is in the Product Backlog. The team
must fulfill or abandon one Product Goal before taking on the next.

### Sprint Backlog and Sprint Goal

The Sprint Backlog is a plan by and for the Developers. It contains:

- The **Sprint Goal**: the single objective for the Sprint and the commitment
  for the Sprint Backlog.
- The Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint.
- An actionable plan for delivering the Increment and achieving the Sprint
  Goal.

The Sprint Backlog is updated throughout the Sprint as more is learned. It
should be detailed enough that Developers can inspect progress in the Daily
Scrum. It is not a fixed contract for completing every forecast item.

The Sprint Goal creates focus and coherence. The selected scope can be
clarified and renegotiated with the Product Owner during the Sprint, but
changes must not endanger the goal.

### Increment and Definition of Done

An Increment is a concrete step toward the Product Goal. Each Increment is
additive to previous Increments and thoroughly verified to ensure all
Increments work together. It must be usable. Work can produce more than one
Increment during a Sprint; their sum is presented at the Sprint Review. An
Increment may be delivered to stakeholders before the end of a Sprint. The
Review is not a release gate.

An Increment must be usable and meet the **Definition of Done**. Work that
does not meet the Definition of Done is not part of the Increment and cannot
be released or presented at the Sprint Review. It returns to the Product
Backlog for future consideration.

The Definition of Done is a formal description of the state of the Increment
when it meets the product's required quality measures. It creates a shared
understanding of what Done means and supports transparency.

- If the organization has a Definition of Done standard, all Scrum Teams
  working on the product must follow it as a minimum.
- If there is no organizational standard, the Scrum Team creates a
  Definition of Done appropriate for the product.
- When multiple Scrum Teams work together on one product, they must share
  the same Definition of Done.

## 6. Important distinctions and common misconceptions

- **The Sprint Goal is the commitment; the forecast is not a promise to finish
  every selected item.** Developers create the forecast and adapt the plan as
  they learn.
- **Scope can change during a Sprint, but the Sprint Goal must not be
  endangered.** The Product Owner and Developers can clarify and renegotiate
  scope.
- **The Product Owner orders the Product Backlog; Developers decide how to
  do the work and make the Sprint forecast.** Planning is collaborative, not
  a handoff.
- **The Daily Scrum belongs to Developers.** It is for adapting their plan
  toward the Sprint Goal, not for reporting status to a manager.
- **The Sprint Review is not an approval meeting or a required demo format.**
  It is a collaborative inspection of the outcome and likely future
  adaptations.
- **A usable Increment does not have to wait for the Sprint Review to be
  released.** Release timing is not prescribed by Scrum.
- **Only work meeting the Definition of Done is part of an Increment.**
  Incomplete work cannot be released or presented at the Sprint Review and
  returns to the Product Backlog for future consideration.
- **Product Backlog refinement is ongoing, not one of the five Scrum events.**
  The Guide assigns it no fixed timebox or percentage of capacity.
- **Scrum does not require user stories, story points, velocity, burndown
  charts, or a specific software-development process.** Teams may use
  suitable techniques, but these are not Scrum requirements.
- **The Scrum Guide does not prescribe an exact event-duration formula for
  Sprints shorter than one month.** The stated event limits are maximums for
  a one-month Sprint; shorter Sprints usually have shorter events.
- **The five Scrum values are not mapped to the three empiricism pillars in
  the Guide.**
- **The Scrum Team is self-managing and cross-functional, but this does not
  mean each person must do every kind of work.** The team collectively has
  the skills it needs and decides how to organize its work.

## 7. A quick way to reason through Scrum scenarios

These prompts are study heuristics for applying the Guide, not additional
Scrum requirements.

When considering a scenario, ask:

1. Which artifact or goal is involved?
2. Is the work transparent enough for the people responsible to inspect it?
3. Who is accountable for the decision or outcome?
4. Does the proposed action help the team achieve its goal without reducing
   quality?
5. Is the team adapting its plan based on what it has learned, while
   respecting the relevant commitment?
6. Is the answer a Scrum requirement, or merely one possible practice?

Keep the three commitments distinct:

- Product Backlog → **Product Goal**
- Sprint Backlog → **Sprint Goal**
- Increment → **Definition of Done**
